# Kenyang grammar, from a published lexicon

Source: the grammatical introduction to a published Kenyang lexicon (1998).
This is the same work the dictionary entries came from.

This is a published description, not speaker-confirmed for this project. Where it
agrees with the verified files it supports them; where it differs, the verified
form is the one to use, and the difference is a question for a speaker. The
spellings here are the book's, including its older conventions.

---

## Word order

Kenyang is a subject-verb-object language.

    Atɔ mmɔ annywɔp          he sent the child to the house
    Mma ako njyɛ            mother is climbing a hill

## How two nouns are joined (possession)

The book confirms the rule your speaker file already has, and explains the
mechanism: between a possessor and the thing possessed there is an associative
marker, often only a tone, sometimes the word ɛ.

    ete e nnɔk              a pot of soup   (pot + marker + soup)
    betok bepay            two villages    (villages + two)

The possessor-first order matches the verified rule: the thing owned and its
marker come in a fixed order, not the English way round.

## Adjectives are few, and agree with the noun class

Kenyang has very few true adjectives. Most descriptions are built another way
(a stative clause, "the stone which is hard", rather than "the hard stone").
The real adjectives are mainly numbers and colours. An adjective takes a prefix
set by the noun's class, and it follows the noun.

    eríti eket             a beautiful house     (class 7)
    beriti beket           beautiful houses      (class 8)
    esaptí enok            a long stick
    besaptí menok          long sticks

This is why "which side of the noun" has an answer: these adjectives go AFTER
the noun, and their prefix changes with the noun.

## The pronoun table

Independent pronouns stand alone (answering "who?"). Possessive pronouns point
at a following noun they possess.

    person      independent        possessive
    I           mme                eya
    you         wo                 eye
    he/she      yi / yo            eyi
    we          bese               eyese
    you (pl)    beka               eyeka
    they        bo                 eyap

This is close to the forms in the EyaLingo material and to your verified
pronouns. Where a form here differs from the verified file, the verified file
wins; the difference is worth checking with a speaker.

## Nouns come in classes, and the class sets the plural

Every noun belongs to a class, shown by its prefix, and the plural is a
different class with a different prefix. This is why the plural is not one rule:
it depends on the class.

    class 1 → 2     mfɔ / bafɔ          chief / chiefs
                    nnɔ / manɔ          mother / mothers
    class 3 → 6     agwa / bagwa        cat / cats
    class 5 → 6     nepɛm / bapɛm       life / lives
    class 6a        manyep              water   (plural-shape mass noun)
    class 7 → 8     etok / betok        village / villages
                    eket / beket        house / houses
    class 9 → 10    nkok / nkok         fowl / fowls (same form)
                    mmen / mmen         goat / goats (same form)
    class 19 → 13   sekwɔp / kekwɔp     spoon / spoons

Two things worth keeping from this:
- Some nouns look the same in singular and plural (fowl, goat). That is correct,
  not a missing plural.
- A plural prefix ba- or be- before a nasal sound becomes nasal itself:
  manyep (water), not banyep; mmen (husbands), not bamen.

## Making a noun from a verb

Put the prefix ne- on almost any verb and you get the "-ing" noun.

    tɔg (pound)     →   nedɔg (pounding)
    rem (say)       →   nerem (saying)
    dɔk (jump)      →   nedɔk (jumping)

## Saying no: three different negatives

This is the fuller version of the negation rule in your speaker file. Kenyang
has THREE negatives, each for a different time or mood.

    pu       present and future      Apu two          he is not coming
                                     Apu chɔg two     he will not come
    bhiki    the past                Abhiki nye       he did not eat
                                     Babhiki ko       they did not walk
    ke       the hortative (should)  Ake two          he should not come
                                     Bake mɔ          they should not try

So "not" is not one word. pu for now and the future, bhiki for the past, ke for
"should not". This matches and extends the á-kɛ́ / pú split already noted in the
texts.

## Tense sits on the verb

Four tenses, shown on the verb two (come):

    near past       atwɔ mbɛ           he came (within a couple of days)
    far past        atwɔ nyaka         he came (long ago)
    future certain  achɔg two          he will come
    future uncertain amay two          he may come

And aspect, how an action is spread in time:

    atwa               he has come
    atwɔ noko          he usually comes      (noko = the habitual)
    itwɔ               he is coming

nyaka for the far past matches the verified past-tense marker. noko for "keeps
doing / usually" matches the verified nɔkɔ.

## Joining words

    ne       and       nkɔk ne nsi        fowl and fish
    ke       then       Atwɔ ke anye       he came, then he ate

## Subordinators (joining a clause)

    mpok     when       Achwe amem eket mpok batwɔ   he entered when they came
    mbɔk     if
    kpate    until      Achɔg choko fa kpate mantwɔ  he'll be here until they come
    be       that       Arem be mandɔk esyɛ          he says that they should go

## Serial verbs: two verbs in a row

Kenyang can put two verbs together where English uses "to" or "and".

    Achɔg rɔŋ esye beku nnya      he will go to the market to buy meat
    Arok etɔk aku nta             he went to town and bought a hat

## Reduplication: saying it twice

Doubling a word changes its meaning. This is the rule behind bataŋ-bataŋ,
ɛrɨ́rɨ́ and the others in the texts.

    ayak (quick)    →   ayak-ayak (very quickly)
    chu (red)       →   chu-chu (flower)
    peti (soft)     →   peti-peti (very soft)
    mme (I)         →   mme-mme (alone)

## Compound nouns are joined with a hyphen

When two nouns join, the writing uses a hyphen. Sometimes a marker sits on the
first noun.

    ebha-manyɛ          courtyard   (fence + case)
    ebhaga-nkok         rooster     (sacrifice + fowl)
    emi-awɔ             wrist       (neck + hand)
    enokɔ-kefɔ          throne      (stick + chiefdom, with marker)
    ekwa-nsurɛ          banana      (plantain + [unidentifiable])
