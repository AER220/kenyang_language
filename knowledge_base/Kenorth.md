## 1. Introduction
The present work describes the orthography of Kenyang, a Bantoid language spoken in the Manyu and Meme Divisions of the South-West Province, Republic of Cameroon.[cite: 19] Kenyang has three main dialects: Upper Kenyang, Lower Kenyang and Kitwii.[cite: 19] Upper Kenyang and Lower Kenyang are situated in Manyu Division, and Kitwii in Meme Division.[cite: 19]

Ittmann (1935/36) published the first research work in Kenyang, producing a grammar and a collection of folktales and proverbs.[cite: 19] In 1983, Tyhurst carried out a sociolinguistic study of Kenyang and Denya and analysed the phonology of Kenyang.[cite: 19] Mbuagbaw and Punches (1990) proposed the first tentative Kenyang alphabet and orthography.[cite: 19] The use of these materials over time by Kenyang speakers showed that there were certain inadequacies in the orthography and certain revisions were needed.[cite: 19] In 1996 the SIL Linguistics Department, together with speakers trained in reading and writing Kenyang, carried out research on the Kenyang tone system.[cite: 19] 

The orthography problems were in two areas: First, there was the problem of marking tone, which the Kenyang speakers found difficult.[cite: 19] Secondly, there were problems concerning how best to represent certain word configurations.[cite: 19] Kenyang literacy students felt that there were too many tone marks to learn and so their teachers requested a reduction in the number of tones that the students had to learn to read.[cite: 19] The teachers wanted only two tone marks, namely the high and rising ones, which could easily be identified by the students.[cite: 19] Faced with this situation, Chris Jackson, Larry Seguin, and I investigated the situation and concluded that the orthography did need revision.[cite: 19]

## 3. Tone
Tyhurst (1984) and Mbuagbaw (1994) identified four basic tones in Kenyang: two level tones, high [´] and low [`], and two contour tones, rising [ˇ] and falling [^].[cite: 19] Tone was represented lexically within morphemes, and grammatically within sentences.[cite: 19]

### 3.1 Tonal Contrast
It is fairly easy to find Kenyang words where the only difference between them is tone.[cite: 19] For example:
(1) 
* [mmú] 'dog' / [mmù] 'person'[cite: 19]
* [ɛβá] 'fence' / [ɛ́βa] 'bag'[cite: 19]
* [ŋkók] 'ladder' / [ŋkòk] 'fowl'[cite: 19]
* [fá] 'give way' / [fà] 'here'[cite: 19]
* [tí] 'to sell' / [fì] 'to scrub'[cite: 19]

### 3.2 Grammatical Functions of Tone
Tone also plays an important role in the grammar of the language.[cite: 19] Compare the following examples:
(2) 
* Present perfective: [àtò] 'he has sent'[cite: 19]
* Present imperfective: [àtó] 'he sent'[cite: 19]
* Present continuous: [àꜜtó] 'he is sending'[cite: 19]

Thus, if we do not mark tone adequately, the meaning can be ambiguous.[cite: 19] The downstep arrow [ꜜ] means that the high tone is lowered.[cite: 19]

### 3.3 Representation of Tone in the Orthography
The grammatical functions of tones discussed above make it necessary to represent them in the Kenyang orthography.[cite: 19] Longacre (1964:136-7), Nida (1964b:26-7), and Wiesemann (1989:16) agree that when tone changes affect verb tenses and pronominal subjects, they should be marked in the orthography.[cite: 19] In the 1990 orthography, tones were marked in the orthography as follows.[cite: 19] Since the low tone has a higher frequency than the high tone, the low tone was left unmarked, following the tone economy principle of Williamson (1984).[cite: 19] The other three tones were marked as high (´), falling (^), and rising (ˇ).[cite: 19]

The Society for Kenyang Literature (SKL) started producing literature in 1990 after the Linguistics Department of SIL, Yaoundé approved the initial tentative orthography.[cite: 19] In 1991, the Canadian International Development Agency (CIDA), financed the first teachers training seminar.[cite: 19] About 55 teachers have now received training on how to teach Kenyang in their respective schools and communities, all using the initial orthography.[cite: 19]

Beginning in 1995, we began receiving feedback from teachers and supervisors.[cite: 19] One of the major problems was that of tone marking.[cite: 19] Many teachers shared the view that they did not see the difference between a downstepped high and a high tone on the one hand, and an unmarked low tone and a falling tone on the other hand.[cite: 19] According to their suggestions, the downstepped high should have been marked as high, while the falling tone should have been left unmarked, like the low tone.[cite: 19] Following these suggestions, however, would cause important verbal contrasts to be neutralised, and thereby increase the level of ambiguity in the written forms.[cite: 19] The reason was that the falling tone and the unmarked low mark a certain contrast in the verb stem.[cite: 19] The same situation occurs between the high tone and the downstepped high.[cite: 19] Here are some examples in (3):

(3) 
* [bátí] 'They sold.' (P1) vs. [báꜜtí] 'They are selling.' (PROG)[cite: 19]
* [máné] 'They ate.' (P1) vs. [máꜜné] 'They are eating.' (PROG)[cite: 19]
* [bákwén] 'They fell.' (P1) vs. [báꜜkwén] 'They are falling.' (PROG)[cite: 19]
* [bátô] 'They threw.' (P1) vs. [bátò] 'They are throwing.' (PROG)[cite: 19]
* [bákɔ̂] 'They walked.' (P1) vs. [bákɔ̀] 'They are walking.' (PROG)[cite: 19]
* [básô] 'They washed.' (P1) vs. [básò] 'They are washing.' (PROG)[cite: 19]

In order to solve these problems, Chris Jackson, an SIL member working with the Manyu team suggested that the grave accent from French could be used to mark the progressive aspect in Kenyang.[cite: 19] We tried this proposal, and it has worked successfully.[cite: 19] Thus, instead of marking four tones, we mark only three and use the grave accent to indicate progressive aspect in the revised orthography.[cite: 19] Teaching the grave accent as the progressive aspect marker causes no problem for people used to the old orthography because the grave accent was previously not used in the old orthography.[cite: 19] Mbuagbaw (1997) introduced this new system of marking tone in Kenyang Primer 1.[cite: 19]

The new system of marking tone and aspect was satisfactory to both the teachers and supervisors.[cite: 19] They remarked that this orthography was easier than the first one because the number of tone marks was reduced from four to three, and the grave accent was only used with the progressive aspect.[cite: 19] Below is a chart contrasting the old and new tone orthographies.[cite: 19]

(4) 
| Tones/Aspect | Old Orthography | New Orthography |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| [´] (High) | ´ | ´ |[cite: 19]
| [ꜜ] (Downstep) | ¯ | ´ |[cite: 19]
| [`] (Low) | (unmarked) | (unmarked) |[cite: 19]
| [ˇ] (Rising) | ˇ | ˇ |[cite: 19]
| [^] (Falling) | ^ | (unmarked) |[cite: 19]
| PROG | ¯ | ` |[cite: 19]

*Table III: Representation of Tone in Old and New Orthographies*[cite: 19]

One of the main changes with the new orthography is that downstepped high tones, indicated by the macron (¯) are replaced by either a high tone (in a non-progressive sentence) or a grave accent (in a progressive sentence), as in (5).[cite: 19]

(5) 
| Old Orthography | New Orthography | Gloss |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Bá ké twō. | Bá ké twó. | 'They should not come.' |[cite: 19]
| A bhí kí nyē. | Abhí kí nyé. | 'He did not eat.' |[cite: 19]
| Mmē rōn ɛtok. | Mmɛ ròŋ ɛtok. | 'I am going to the village.' |[cite: 19]
| A nā nɛnyé. | A nà nɛnyé. | 'He is cooking food.' |[cite: 19]

Another important change is that falling tones are now left unmarked (like low tones), as in (6) below.[cite: 19]

(6) 
| Old Orthography | New Orthography | Gloss |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Sé rô kɛbhi. | Sé rok ɛbhi. | 'We went to the bush.' |[cite: 19]
| Bǎ sô nden. | Bǎ so nden. | 'You washed clothes.' |[cite: 19]

As mentioned above, the overwhelming response to these changes in writing tones has been positive.[cite: 19]

## 4. Word Division
Articles, negation markers, tense markers, and modal auxiliaries were written separately in the 1990 orthography as they are in English, and no attempt was made to determine the orthographic word in Kenyang from the native speaker's point of view.[cite: 19] Certain problems arose as to whether certain "clitic type" elements had the status of a word or not.[cite: 19]

### 4.1 Subject Marker
In the new orthography, the subject marker is attached to whatever follows it because linguistically, the subject marker is a verbal prefix similar in nature to the noun class prefix on nouns.[cite: 19] Compare the following examples in the new and old orthography:[cite: 19]

(7) 
| Old orthography | New orthography | Gloss |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Má ń nê mmɔ. | Má nne mmɔ. | 'Let them feed the child.' |[cite: 19]
| Bá bhí kí twó ɛyú. | Bá bhí kí twó ɛyú. | 'They did not come yesterday.' |[cite: 19]
| Sé ńdó ketok. | Sé ndó ketok. | 'Let us go to the village.' |[cite: 19]
| Manko bá pú fâ. | Manko bá pú fa. | 'There are no strangers.' |[cite: 19]

The Hortative marker, (in addition to the subject marker) is attached to the verb in the new orthography.[cite: 19]

### 4.2 Noun Phrase
The noun phrase may consist of a head noun, followed by modifiers such as adjectives and/or determiners.[cite: 19] Noun phrases also include NP-NP constructions in which the first NP is "possessed" by the second NP.[cite: 19] In Kenyang, this construction is marked by an associative marker between the possessor and the possessed.[cite: 19] Typical noun phrases are as follows:[cite: 19]

(8) 
| N | ADJ | N | N |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| e-ket | ɛ pέpέp | e-te | é nnɔk |[cite: 19]
| 7-house | AM white | 7-pot | AM 5-soup |[cite: 19]
| | 'chief's village' | 'pot of soup' | |[cite: 19]

#### 4.2.1 Associative Phrase
The associative phrase marker (henceforth AP) is a vowel in Kenyang.[cite: 19] For some noun classes it copies the final vowel of the first noun, and for other noun classes, the vowel is different.[cite: 19] The tone of the AP is either H or L, depending on the noun class of the head noun.[cite: 19] In both the tentative and revised orthographies, the AP is not written because the native speaker reads easily (automatically inserting the correct vowel and tone) even when they are not included, as below:[cite: 19]

(9) 
| Linguistic Forms | Orthography | Gloss |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| mpòkó n-tóŋ (1-time AM 1-teacher) | mpok ntoŋ | 'teacher's time' |[cite: 19]
| bètôkɔ́ bé-pây (8-village AM 8-two) | betok bé pay | 'two villages' |[cite: 19]

#### 4.2.2 Compound Nouns
In some compound nouns, both words are identifiable as individual nouns while in others, one or more of the nouns is not identifiable by itself.[cite: 19] In the orthography, the components of all compound nouns are separated by hyphens.[cite: 19] We now look at several types.[cite: 19]

The major type of compound nouns we will look at are noun-noun sequences, in which the second noun is attributive.[cite: 19] In the orthography, for compound forms that do not take the normal associative marker the hyphen goes between the two nouns.[cite: 19] Here are some examples (all of the initial words being vowel final).[cite: 19]

(10) 
| Linguistic Forms | Orthographic Forms | Gloss |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| è-bhá (7-fence) + mà-nyé (6-case) | ɛbhá-manyé | 'courtyard' |[cite: 19]
| e-bhá (7-sacrifice) + n-kôk (9-fowl) | ɛbhá-nkok | 'rooster' |[cite: 19]
| n-chyề (1-giver) + sòrì (7-trouble) | nchye-esonori | 'nuisance' |[cite: 19]
| èmì (7-neck) + à-wó (3-hand) | emi-awó | 'wrist' |[cite: 19]
| m-mù (1-person) + m-fáy (5-top) | mmŭ-mfáy | 'people from the grassfields' |[cite: 19]

There are a number of compound nouns for which one of the roots is not identifiable in isolation.[cite: 19] It is possible that some of these nouns were borrowed from neighbouring languages.[cite: 19] In the orthography, the identifiable nouns and those not identifiable are separated by hyphens in the same manner as nouns that are identifiable.[cite: 19] *(Note: In the examples below, '??' signifies that the original historical meaning of the specific bound root is unknown).*[cite: 19]

(11) 
* á-ghó nò (3-??) + m-mέn (9-goat) -> **ághóno-mmέn** 'sheep'[cite: 19]
* àmàn (3-kernel) + kárárá (??) -> **aman-kárárá** 'coconut'[cite: 19]
* é-kwá (7-plantain) + nsurέ (5-??) -> **ékwá-nsurέ** 'banana'[cite: 19]

For compound nouns that take an associative marker, the hyphen goes between the associative marker and the second noun.[cite: 19]

(12) 
* e-nòk (7-stick) + kè-fɔ (13-chiefdom) -> **ɛnɔkó-kefɔ** 'throne'[cite: 19]
* m-pɔ̀ (9-cow) + n-dík (1-whiteman) -> **mpɔnɔ-ndík** 'horse'[cite: 19]
* n-dèn (5-cloth) + bè-kók (8-bed) -> **ndené-bekók** 'sheet'[cite: 19]
* àmàn (3-kernel) + nɛbù (5-sky) -> **amaná-nebu** 'hail stone'[cite: 19]
* bhák ('to be') + mè-nwòp (8-heavy) -> **bháká-menwop** 'important'[cite: 19]
* e-nyì (7-thing) + bèt (??) -> **enyiní-bet** 'weapon'[cite: 19]
* m-bòk (5-hole) + bàchè (6-urine) -> **mbokó-bache** 'seminal canal'[cite: 19]

### 4.3 Reduplication
Reduplication has a range of forms and functions in Kenyang.[cite: 19] There are situations where reduplication is derivational and in that case, a new word is formed.[cite: 19] In the orthography, reduplicated words are separated by hyphens:[cite: 19]

(13) 
* áyák 'quick' -> **áyák-áyák** 'very quickly'[cite: 19]
* bínírí 'round' -> **bínírí-bínírí** 'become round'[cite: 19]
* chu 'red' -> **chu-chu** 'flower'[cite: 19]
* esa 'twenty' -> **esa-esa** 'four hundred'[cite: 19]
* peti 'soft' -> **peti-peti** 'very soft'[cite: 19]
* ntí 'head' -> **ntí-ntí** 'bony'[cite: 19]
* mme 'I' -> **mme-mme** 'alone'[cite: 19]
* ngó 'fire' -> **ngó-ngó** 'full of fire'[cite: 19]

### 4.4 Verb Phrase
The imperative is taken as the citation form of the verb in this study.[cite: 19] The verb in Kenyang does not have a prefix or a suffix in its citation, or basic form.[cite: 19] The verb root in Kenyang is either monosyllabic, disyllabic or trisyllabic.[cite: 19]

Kenyang distinguishes four tenses: near past, far past, future certain, and future uncertain; and four aspect markers: present perfective, present imperfective, present habitual, and the progressive.[cite: 19] In the orthography, the tense/aspect markers are written separately if they occur after the verb.[cite: 19] If they occur before the verb, they are written together with the preceding subject marker.[cite: 19] The examples in 4.4.1 below illustrate this:[cite: 19]

#### 4.4.1 Tense
The verb nisí 'to refuse' is used to illustrate the orthography for the various kinds of tense marking, as below:[cite: 19]

(14) 
| Tense | Linguistic Forms | Orthographic Forms | Gloss |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Near past | A-nísí-mbú (3S-refuse-P1) | **Anísí mbú.** | 'He refused recently.' |[cite: 19]
| Far past | A-nísí-nyáká (3S-refuse-P2) | **Anísí nyáka.** | 'He refused a long time ago.' |[cite: 19]
| Future (certain) | A-choŋ-nìsì (3S-F2-refuse) | **A choŋ nisi.** | 'He will refuse.' |[cite: 19]
| Future (uncertain) | A-măy-nìsì (3S-F1-refuse) | **Amǎy nisi.** | 'He may refuse.' |[cite: 19]

#### 4.4.2 Aspect
All of the aspect markers are shown below with the verb nisí 'to refuse' in the present tense.[cite: 19]

(15) 
| Aspect | Linguistic Forms | Orthographic Forms | Gloss |

#### 4.4.3 Infinitive
The infinitival prefix in Kenyang has two allomorphs, bé- and mé, which are attached to the verb root.[cite: 19] mé occurs before verb roots that begin with a nasal consonant while bé- occurs elsewhere.[cite: 19] In the orthography, both forms are written as they are pronounced and they are written attached to the verb.[cite: 19]

(16) 
| Linguistic Forms | Orthographic Forms | Gloss |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| mé-nísi (INF-refuse) | **ménísi** | 'to refuse' |[cite: 19]
| bé-pá (INF-spit) | **bépá** | 'to spit' |[cite: 19]
| bé-tô (INF-throw) | **béto** | 'to throw' |[cite: 19]
| mé-nyókoti (INF-gather) | **ményókoti** | 'to gather' |[cite: 19]

#### 4.4.4 Negation
There are three types of negation in Kenyang.[cite: 19] Pú marks negation in the present and future tenses, ke marks negation in the hortative mood, and bhíkí marks negation in the past tense.[cite: 19] In the orthography, both the negative marker and the preceding subject marker are represented as one word.[cite: 19] Here are some examples:[cite: 19]

(17) 
| Linguistic Forms | Orthographic Forms | Gloss |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A-pú-twó (3s-NEG-come) | **Apú twó.** | 'He is not coming.' |[cite: 19]
| A-pú-choŋ-twó (3S-NEG-FUT-come) | **Apú choŋ twó.** | 'He will not come.' |[cite: 19]

Bhíkí marks negation in the past tense.[cite: 19] Here are some examples:[cite: 19]

(18) 
| Linguistic Forms | Orthography Forms | Gloss |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A-bhíkí-nyé (3S-NEG-eat) | **Abhíkí nyé.** | 'He did not eat.' |[cite: 19]
| Bá-bhíkí-ko (3P-NEG-walk) | **Bá bhíkí ko.** | 'They did not walk.' |[cite: 19]

Ke marks negation in the hortative mood.[cite: 19]

(19) 
| Linguistic Form | Orthographic Form | Gloss |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A-ké-twó (3S-NEG-come) | **A ké twó.** | 'He should not come.' |[cite: 19]

### 4.5 Conjunctions and Subordinators
The conjunctions nɛ and ke join equivalent structures such as noun phrases and verb phrases.[cite: 19] In the orthography, they are written as separate words.[cite: 19]

(20) 
| Linguistic Form | Orthographic Form | Gloss |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| nkok ne nsi (fowl and fish) | **nkok ne nsi** | 'fowl and fish' |[cite: 19]
| nta ne ekáti (cap and book) | **nta ne ekáti** | 'cap and book' |[cite: 19]
| Atwó ke anyé (3S-came then 3S-ate) | **Atwó ke anyé** | 'He came then he ate.' |[cite: 19]

Subordinators link dependent and independent clauses.[cite: 19] There are two types of subordinators, namely, subordinating conjunctions and subordinating adverbs.[cite: 19] The subordinating conjuctions in Kenyang are: mpok 'when', mbák 'if', kpáte 'until', and bé 'that'.[cite: 19] Subordinating adverbs include: kɛ 'however', and yenɔ 'nevertheless.'[cite: 19]

Subordinating adverbs are always followed by a comma in the orthography.[cite: 19] The comma in this case shows that a pause is obligatory.[cite: 19]

(21)
* **Kɛ, bá pú twó mbwóré.** 'However, they will not come tomorrow.'[cite: 19]
* **Yenɔ, mfɔ ă choŋ twó.** 'Nevertheless, the chief will come.'[cite: 19]

In the examples of subordinating conjunctions below, there is no comma, thus indicating that there is no obligatory pause.[cite: 19]

(22)
* **Achwe amem eket mpok bá twó annywóp.** 'He entered the house when they came.'[cite: 19]
* **Arem bé má ndó kesyé.** 'He says that they should go to the market.'[cite: 19]
* **Mmeyá riŋi mbák yí achí.** 'I want to know whether he is at home.'[cite: 19]
* **A choŋ choko fa kpáte má ntwó.** 'He will be here until they come.'[cite: 19]

## 5. Punctuation and Capitalisation
Most, if not, all modern orthographies include punctuation marks.[cite: 19] Unfortunately, in many literacy efforts, little effort is made to teach the functions and significance of each punctuation mark.[cite: 19] The reason for this is perhaps that they are regarded to be universal.[cite: 19] Gleason (1955:432) remarks that "people do not expect to find differences in punctuation from language to language."[cite: 19] Nevertheless, languages do differ sometimes in how they treat punctuation (e.g English vs French), and so we will now look at the conventions for punctuation in Kenyang.[cite: 19]

The punctuation marks and the rules that govern them in Kenyang are the same as those for English.[cite: 19] The punctuation marks used for Kenyang are therefore as follows:
1) Full stop (.)[cite: 19]
2) Question mark (?)[cite: 19]
3) Exclamation mark (!)[cite: 19]
4) Comma (,)[cite: 19]
5) Colon (:)[cite: 19]
6) Semicolon (;)[cite: 19]
7) Quotation marks ("")[cite: 19]

### 5.1 Full Stop
The full stop (.) in Kenyang marks the end of a declarative sentence:[cite: 19]
(23) 
**Bá ko ne bekak.**
3P.walk with legs
'They are walking on foot.'[cite: 19]

### 5.2 Question Mark
The question mark (?) marks the end of an interrogative sentence:[cite: 19]
(24) 
**Má nyi aku yi esyé?**
Manyi 3S.buy.P1 what market
'Manyi bought what in the market?'[cite: 19]

**Aku nnya ne bako.**
3S.buy meat and beans
'She bought meat and beans.'[cite: 19]

### 5.3 Exclamation Mark
The exclamation mark marks either the end of a command, as in (25a), or is placed following exclamatory words or phrases, as in (25b).[cite: 19] It functions to express interjections, surprises, excitement, and forceful comments, as below.[cite: 19]

(25a) 
**Dók!**
go (imp.)
'Go!'[cite: 19]

**Aké twó!**
3S.NEG come
'He should not come!'[cite: 19]

(25b) 
**Chá!**
Chá expresses negative surprise, especially when one is accused of doing something of which he is unaware.[cite: 19] If there is an exclamation in the middle of a sentence, note that the word which follows the exclamation sign begins with a capital letter, as it is a new sentence.[cite: 19]

(26) 
**Nkwá ne! Pú ngátí mbú wo?**
EX NEG 1S.tell P1 you
'Nkwá ne! (expresses a call to attention) Did I not tell you?'[cite: 19]

### 5.4 Comma
Commas are used to indicate where a pause occurs or may occur in natural speech.[cite: 19] In oral communication, pauses always occur following certain grammatical units such as conjunctive adverbs.[cite: 19] The authors should note such pauses in a written text by inserting commas.[cite: 19] In Kenyang, the comma (,) serves several purposes:[cite: 19]

**Words and Phrases**
Commas set off conjunctive adverbs that qualify or change the emphasis of a sentence.[cite: 19]
(27) **Kɛ, mfɔ apú choŋ twó**
however chief 3S.NEG FUT come
'However, the chief will not come.'[cite: 19]

Commas also set off contrasting words and phrases, no matter where they occur in a sentence.[cite: 19]
(28) **Tambi, pú Tambe, ke á gú annyéŋ.**
Tambi NEG Tambe that 3S.die at.water
'It was Tambi, not Tambe, that died in the stream.'[cite: 19]

(29) **Bɛsé pú choko fa, sé choko chí awu.**
3P.NEG stay here 3P.stay FOC there
'We do not live here, we live over there.'[cite: 19]

(30) **Acha mamyé esa, abhak chu, nɛ asap.**
3S.surpass years twenty 3S.be red and 3S.tall
'He is more than twenty years old, red in colour, and tall.'[cite: 19]

Commas set off relative clauses.[cite: 19] In Kenyang, there is no difference between restrictive and non-restrictive relative clauses.[cite: 19]
(31) **Mmu ane, á twó fa ɛyú, amè.**
person who 3S.come here yesterday 3S.sick
'The man, who came here yesterday, is sick.'[cite: 19]

(32) **Etok ɛnέ, nchókɔ́, chí Tali.**
village where stay.P1 FOC. Tali
'The village, where I stayed, was Tali.'[cite: 19]

Commas set off adverbial phrases and clauses when they occur at the beginning of sentences:[cite: 19]
(33) **Mpok sé gháká annyéŋ. Ndekway, Tambi ne Tanyi bá rok**
when 2P.arrive.P1 Ndekwai Tambi and Tanyi 3P.go.P1 INF.stream
'When we arrived Ndekwai, Tambi and Tanyi went to the stream.'[cite: 19]

after a spatio-temporal location tɛntɛp nyáká (formerly),[cite: 19]
(34) **Tɛntɛp nyáká, nnya é chí nyáká kemwere.**
formerly P2 animals it.FOC P2 friendship
'Formerly, animals were friends.'[cite: 19]

after nnywɔp enénfú (one day),[cite: 19]
(35) **Nnywɔp enénfú, Tambe arok kentémé.**
day certain Tambe 3S.go hunting
'One day, Tambe went hunting.'[cite: 19]

and after nkwó (also) at the beginning of a sentence,[cite: 19]
(36) **Nkwó, mpok bá só nden, bá chye bó nɛnyé**
also when 3P.wash.P1 clothes 3P.give.P1 them food
'Also, when they had finished washing clothes, they gave them food.'[cite: 19]

### 5.5 Colon and Semicolon
Webster's Standard American Style Manual explains that a colon (:) introduces clauses that explain, illustrate, or restate what has gone before.[cite: 19] It is also used in dialogue or following a brief heading or an introductory term.[cite: 19]

(37) **Akɔŋ ɛnyin émot: nɛnyé.**
3S.like thing one food
'She loves one thing: food.'[cite: 19]

The semicolon, on the other hand, pulls related sentences together.[cite: 19] Here are examples with a semicolon:[cite: 19]

When it pivots a contrast:[cite: 19]
(38) **Ku betik mpok betik; ntok mpok ntok.**
do work time work play time play
'Work when it is time to work; play when it is time to play.'[cite: 19]

between related independent clauses when the connection is omitted:[cite: 19]
(39) **Aná erítí nenyé éyú; ɛnέ echon é ri échá.**
3S.cook.P1 good food yesterday one today it.good it.surpass
'He prepared a nice meal yesterday; the one today is better.'[cite: 19]

### 5.6 Quotation Marks
Quotation marks ("") occur at the beginning and end of direct speech.[cite: 19] It has been argued that quotation marks are not needed in many African languages because some languages already employ quotative markers.[cite: 19] Though this is true, quotative marks only introduce the quotation.[cite: 19] Koffi (1995:8) claims that there is no language where there is a lexical element which indicates the end of quotations.[cite: 19] If one relies on quotation openers only, one will not necessarily know when a given quotation ends.[cite: 19] To avoid such problems, it is better to use quotation marks that clearly indicate the beginning and end of quoted discourse, as below:[cite: 19]

(40) **Abhéb, "O fu fá?"**
3S.ask.P1 2P.come.out where
'He asked, "Where are you from?'[cite: 19]

Quotation marks also set off special words, such as those that are not normally part of the language.[cite: 19]

(41) **Enyin ɛnέ barík bá hiŋi bé "rédyo."**
thing that whitemen 3P.call that radio
'The thing that whitemen call "radio".'[cite: 19]

If the quotation needs a punctuation mark (question mark, exclamation mark, etc), it occurs before the last quotation mark.[cite: 19]

(42) **Abik bé, "Twó fa!"**
3S.scream.Pl that come here
'He screamed, "Come here!"'[cite: 19]

In general, all punctuations go inside quotations.[cite: 19]

### 5.7 Capitalisation
Capital letters are used at the beginning of sentences, proper nouns, and after a colon.[cite: 19]
The first word in a declarative, interrogative, or exclamatory sentence is always capitalised, as below:[cite: 19]

First word in a sentence:[cite: 19]
(43a) **Bá róŋ ɛsyé beku ebhen.**
3P.go market evening this
'They are going to the market this evening.'[cite: 19]

proper nouns:[cite: 19]
(44) **Tambi arok ɛkáti abhini: Nsó, Ako, Nkwa, nɛ Tíku.**
Tambi 3S.go school 3S.call.P1 Nso Ako Nkwa and Tiku
'Tambi went to school and called the following: Nso, Ako, Nkwa, and Tiku.'[cite: 19]

after a colon:[cite: 19]
(45) **Ebhép é chí bé: Atwó ɛyú ke abhíkí twó?**
question it.FOC that 3S.come.P1 yesterday or 3S.NEG come.Pl
'The question is: Did the chief come yesterday or not?'[cite: 19]

The first word in a direct quotation is always capitalised.[cite: 19]
(46) **Mfɔ achyɛ ɛyoŋ: "Ye-mmú á ké fú nɛfí."**
chief 3P.give voice no-person 3S.NEG come.out outside
'The chief commanded, "Nobody should go outside."'[cite: 19]

Names, titles, days of the week, are capitalised in Kenyang as follows:[cite: 19]
(47a) **Afu Mámfé.**
3S.come.from Mamfe
'He/She is from Mamfe.'[cite: 19]

b) **Nnyέn ɛni chí Etá Tambe.**
name his COP Mr. Tambe
'His name is Mr. Tambe.'[cite: 19]

c) **A twò nnywɔp Chókóchókó.**
3S.come day Sunday
'He is coming on Sunday.'[cite: 19]

### 5.8 Summary
There are two main areas of change in the revised orthography.[cite: 19] The first is the tonal change (discussed under section 3.3.1).[cite: 19] The present system of marking tone reduces the number of tones which the speaker has to mark.[cite: 19] The second area of change concerns the "clitic type" subject morphemes, which are written as prefixes on whichever word follows (discussed under section 4.1).[cite: 19] All other conventions discussed in this paper remain the same as in the original tentative orthography.[cite: 19]
