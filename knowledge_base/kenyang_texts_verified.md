# Kenyang texts, confirmed by a native speaker

Whole pieces of real Kenyang speech, each line with its meaning. Confirmed by me,
Agbor Edouard Ransome, a native Kenyang speaker from Bachou Ntai.

Connected speech teaches what single phrases cannot. A word list gives pieces; a
text shows how one idea joins to the next, how a command softens into a wish, and
how a speaker addresses the dead and the living in the same breath.

Everything in this file is correct Kenyang and can be taught as it stands.

# Text 1: A meeting at Arrey

What is said at a village meeting when the drink is poured and the ancestors are
called on.

## The first pouring

    Ghó ɛchɛmɛ ɛnɛ́ ɛ́chémtí.
    People are already gathered for the meeting

    Mántwó nɛ mmɛm bataŋ-bataŋ.
    let them come with strong drinks

    Kɛ mánáŋ bátwɔ nɛ mɔ́.
    but they have already come with it

    bɛ́ ɛchɛmɛ ɛnɛ ɛ́nkɔ pɛtii.
    so that this meeting will be a success

    Bɔ ɛ́tá, ghó ka bo abhɛ́n bátwɔ́
    Our forefathers, look at those who have already come

    bɛtɔkɔ́-bɛtɔk ndu ɛchɛmɛ ɛnɛ́n nɛ.
    from different villages because of this meeting

    Ghó awɔ́-ghɔ anɛ́ mmɛ nchí fyɛ́
    this is my left hand I am putting in

    Mánsɔt mmɛm amɛn mánnyú
    let them take these drinks and drink

    Ɛchɛmɛ ɛnɛ ɛ́ndɔ́k nɔ́kɔ́ pɛtii
    let this meeting be a success

## If someone brings trouble

    Mmu anɛ áfú ɛyi ɛtɔk,
    If somebody comes from his village

    ábhɔ́ŋ ndak nɛ ntɨ
    and has a problem with somebody

    kɛ yí bɛ́ ámbírɛ́ fa yí ɛ́nchɔ́ŋtí nnyɛ́n Are
    he plans in his heart to blackmail the name of Arrey

    bɛ́ ɛsɔŋɔri ɛnɛ ɛ́mfú chí fa Are
    that this problem should come from Arrey

    awɔ́-ghɔ ánsɔt nɔkɔ yí
    the left hand should take him

    Mámbɛp nɔkɔ yí bɛ́
    they will be asking him

    ɛtɔk Are kɛ ɛ́bhɔ́ŋ ɛsɔŋɔri?
    the village of Arrey hasn't a problem

    Ɔku na kɛ ɔpɔkɔ ɛsɔŋɔri ɔtwɔ́ fa nɛ yɔ́?
    what happened before you brought this problem to this village?

    Ghó awɔ́-nɛ́m aywɛsɛ́ sɛ́chí wɛn
    This is our right hand of those of us who are here

## The drinking begins

    Sɛ́twó bhɔ mmɛm mɛ́nyú
    We are going to start drinking

    Sɛ́nku sɛ́mbɛ́n babhɛ́n
    let us dance

    Sɛ́ndɛm kɛpɨ
    let us talk

    Mănto nɔkɔ manyɛp akwɛn
    You people should be pouring cold water

    Ndu nɛchɔkɔ ɛnɛsɛ́ nɛ́chɔ́kɔ́ fa nɛ́nɛ
    in the meeting we are gathered here

    mănáŋ ḿbú ányu amɛka mmɛm
    You have taken your own drink

    Bátwɔ́ bhɔ ɛchɛmɛ
    the meeting is going to start

    Ghó mbɔk mmɛm arɔp amɛn sɛ́twó nkwɔ́ nyú.
    some drinks have remained that we are going to drink also

## The closing words

    Sɛ́rɛm fa ndu ɛchɛmɛ bɛ́ sɛ́mbák ɛyɔŋ ɛ́mɔt.
    We are saying that in this meeting, we should be of one voice

    Yɛ̌ mpútí ákɛ́ bhák arɛ́.
    there should be no arguments

    Ntí ákɛ́ sáy mmú
    nobody should have any head ache

    nɛ awɔ́-nɛ́m ámbak nɔkɔ aywɛsɛ ndu ɛtɔk Are nɛ.
    Let our right hand be with us here in Arrey

# What Text 1 teaches

## The marker in front of the verb says who, and says should

A short marker joins to the front of the verb. It carries both the person and the
sense of *should* or *let*. These are the forms this text gives, each one with the
meaning it has here.

**Let them, they should**

    Mántwó              let them come
    Mánsɔt              let them take
    Mánnyú              let them drink
    Mámbɛp nɔkɔ yí      they will be asking him

**Let him, he should**

    ánsɔt nɔkɔ yí       the left hand should take him
    ámbak nɔkɔ          it should keep being
    ákɛ́ bhák            it should not be
    ámbírɛ́              he plans

**Let it, it should**

    ɛ́nkɔ pɛtii          it will be a success
    ɛ́ndɔ́k nɔ́kɔ́        let it be
    ɛ́mfú               it should come
    ɛ́bhɔ́ŋ              it has
    ɛ́nchɔ́ŋtí           to spoil, to blackmail

**Let us, we should**

    sɛ́mbák             we should be
    sɛ́chí              we are
    Sɛ́twó              we are going to
    Sɛ́ndɛm             let us talk
    Sɛ́nku sɛ́mbɛ́n      let us dance

**You people should**

    Mănto nɔkɔ          you people should be pouring

This joins the forms already in this file: bɔ́ mántɨ́knsɛm, they should repent, and
mmá amfoŋori, mother should forgive.

## náŋ: already, have done

    Kɛ mánáŋ bátwɔ nɛ mɔ́      but they have already come with it
    mănáŋ ḿbú ányu            you have already taken

This is the same naŋ already in this file, meaning to finish, and Ánáŋ, done.
Put on the front of a verb it says the thing is already done.

## Three ways of saying no

Each has its own job and they are not interchangeable.

    pú        not, in a plain statement       mme pú rong
    Kɛ́        do not, as a command            Kɛ́ dɔ́k
    á-kɛ́      should not                      ákɛ́ bhák

From the text:

    Yɛ̌ mpútí ákɛ́ bhák arɛ́       there should be no argument
    Ntí ákɛ́ sáy mmú              no one should have a headache

## ghó: here is, look

Ghó opens a line to point at something, the way a hand points.

    Ghó ɛchɛmɛ ɛnɛ́ ɛ́chémtí      look, this meeting is gathered
    Ghó ka bo abhɛ́n bátwɔ́       look at those who have come
    Ghó awɔ́-ghɔ anɛ́             here is this left hand
    Ghó mbɔk mmɛm arɔp           here is the drink that is left

## This changes shape with the noun

The word for *this* is not one word. It answers the noun it sits with.

    ɛchɛmɛ ɛnɛ́              this meeting
    bo abhɛ́n                these people
    mmɛm amɛn               these drinks
    Mmu anɛ                 the person who

## Saying it twice makes it stronger

    bataŋ-bataŋ             strong, of drink
    bɛtɔkɔ́-bɛtɔk            village after village, different villages

## ndu: in, and because of

    ndu ɛchɛmɛ ɛnɛ́n nɛ      because of this meeting
    Ndu nɛchɔkɔ ɛnɛsɛ́       in the meeting

With ndǔ already in this file for *as* and *during*, this word covers in, during,
as, and because of. The sense comes from what follows it.

## nɔkɔ keeps its place

nɔkɔ comes straight after the verb and means to keep doing. The text confirms it
with four different people:

    ánsɔt nɔkɔ yí           should keep taking him
    Mámbɛp nɔkɔ yí          keep asking him
    Mănto nɔkɔ manyɛp       keep pouring water
    ámbak nɔkɔ aywɛsɛ       should keep being with us

## yɛ̌: not a single one

    Yɛ̌ mpútí ákɛ́ bhák arɛ́       there should be no argument at all

With yɛ̌ mmu, no one, already in this file: yɛ̌ in front of a noun, with a negative
on the verb, says not a single one of these.

## Words this text confirms in use

    ɛchɛmɛ      meeting             nnyɛ́n       name
    mmɛm        drink, wine         ɛtɔk        village, town
    awɔ́-ghɔ     left hand           ntɨ         heart
    awɔ́-nɛ́m    right hand          ɛsɔŋɔri     trouble, problem
    ndak        problem             nɛ́nɛ        now
    ɛ́tá        father              kɛ          but
    bɛ́         that                ntí         head

# Text 2: The coming of the dry season

What is said when harmattan is on its way. The speaker warns the village, calls
on the ancestors to hold sickness off the children, then turns to the good the
dry season brings, and ends asking protection for those who work the farms.

## The warning

    Ɛtɔk nkɛm ghók yɛ́ka.
    People of the village, listen

    Ɛyú sɛ́ghók bɛ́ nɛbháŋ nɛ́twó.
    Yesterday, we heard that harmattan is coming

    Bǎríŋí bɛ́ nɛbháŋ nɛ́twó nɔ́kɔ́ bó nɛ bɛsɔŋɔri.
    You know that it is harmattan that brings sickness to children

    Bɔ bábhɔ́ŋ mbwɛ́p.
    Children are sick of fever

    Bɔ báchay-nnya.
    They are sick of convulsion

## Calling on the women who dance

    Kɛ sɛ́bhɨŋɨ beka
    But, we are calling you

    abhɛ́n baghɔrɛ abhɛ́n ɛgba.
    you women who dance egba dance

    Yɛ chí nɛme nɛ́twó, nɛ́ndɔp chí ambi.
    If sickness is coming, let it remain on the road

    Ndu nɛbháŋ nɛ́twó nɛ
    As harmattan is approaching

    mbwɛ́p ákɛ́ kɛ́m mmɔ́.
    a child should not be sick of fever

    Ndu nɛbháŋ nɛ́twó nɛ,
    As harmattan is approaching

    bákɛ́ chay-nnya
    let them not be sick of convulsion

    Ndu nɛbháŋ nɛ́twó nɛ,
    As harmattan is coming

    nɛ́nchɔ́kɔ́ nɛ bɛsɛ́ pɛtii,
    let it live with us in peace

    nɛ́nchɔ́kɔ́ nɛ bɛsɛ́ ɛrɨ́rɨ́
    let it live with us well

## What the dry season brings

    Mbák chí ɛnɛm,
    If it is dry season

    yɛ chí ɛnɛm ɛ́twó nɛ
    as dry season is approaching

    mpok maŋák
    a time of happiness

    mpok babhɛ́n
    a time to dance

    mpok bɛgba
    a time to dance egba

    mpok bɛ́kʉ bawú atwɔ
    a time for death celebrations

    mpok bɛ́rɔ́ŋ ɛbhɨ bɛ́rɔ́ŋ tɛ́m bagyɛ́p
    a time to dig palms to prune palm wine

    bɛ́yáŋ mmɛm mɛ́nyú
    to look for wine to drink

    mpok bɛsɛ́ rɔ́ŋ ɛbhɨ bɛ́tɛ́m nnya
    a time for hunting

    mpok bɛsɛ́ rɔ́ŋ fyɛ́ kɛndó annyɛ́n
    a time to catch fish in the river

    mpok bɛ́pók nsi
    a time to bale out water to catch fish

## Asking protection for the farmers

    Ghók yɛ́ka
    Listen, those of you

    ɛnɛm ɛ́twɔ.
    Dry season has come

    Mbaŋ áwɔ
    places are dry

    bati baki pɛtii
    let farmers be protected

    mpok básɔ́ŋ mbaŋ
    When they are burning the farms

    mmɔ ákɛ́ bhɔ́ŋ ɛsɔŋɔri
    no child should have any problem

    barɛ́m mǎŋgok nɔkɔ
    ancestors, you people should be listening

    sɛ́bhɨŋɨ beka
    we are calling you people

    ndu ɛ̌tí bɛsɛ́ abhɛ́n sɛ́chí chókɔ́-chókɔ́
    because of those of us who are still alive

    yɛ chí nkwɔ́ ɛywɛka abhɛ́n báyámbɨ
    even those of you who have gone ahead of us

    sɛ́bhɨŋɨ beka bɛ́,
    we are calling on you that

    yɛ chí ngɔrɛ kɛ árɔ́ŋ ɛbhɨ
    if a woman goes to farm

    yɛ chí mbakanɛm
    if a man goes to farm

    yɛ bátítí
    even if they are clearing

    bákwaŋa kwaŋ,
    or they are hoeing

    bákɛ́ kɔt ɛkak
    let them not wound their leg

    kɛ́kɔt sɛnɔŋ-áwɔ́.
    let them not wound their finger

    Bápɨ́ nɔ́kɔ́ nɛnyɛ́,
    when they plant food

    nɛ́ngo nɔkɔ
    let the food grow

## The closing words

    Beka abhɛ́n báchí awu antɛn
    Those of you who are below

    mǎnchɨk nɔkɔ nɛnyɛ́ ɛnɛn bɛ́ nɛ́ngɔ́
    you should protect the crops growing

    mbɔ nɛnyɛ́ ɛnɛ́n nɛ́chí ɛbhɨ nkɨ nsay
    like crops in a hungry farm

    chí beka kɛ bǎyɛ́t.
    it is you people that bless the crops

    Sɛ́káká beka.
    We are grateful to you

    bǎghok ɛnyunɛ bɛsɛ́ rɛ́mɛ́.
    you have heard as we have said

# What Text 2 teaches

## The tone on the marker says whether it is them or you

This is the rule Text 1 only hinted at. The letters are the same; the mark over
the vowel changes who is being spoken to.

    high tone     bá-, mán-      they
    rising tone   bǎ-, mǎn-      you people

Every example across both texts, with no exception:

    bábhɔ́ŋ mbwɛ́p           they are sick of fever
    báchay-nnya             they are sick of convulsion
    bákɛ́ kɔt ɛkak           let them not wound their leg
    Bápɨ́ nɔ́kɔ́ nɛnyɛ́       when they plant food
    bátítí                  they are clearing
    Mántwó                  let them come
    Mánsɔt                  let them take

    Bǎríŋí bɛ́               you know that
    bǎyɛ́t                   you bless
    bǎghok                  you have heard
    Mănto nɔkɔ              you should be pouring
    mǎŋgok nɔkɔ             you should be listening
    mǎnchɨk nɔkɔ            you should protect

So the tone mark is not decoration. Write bá- when you mean them and bǎ- when
you mean you people, or you have said the wrong thing.

## The let marker answers the noun

The marker that means *let* or *should* changes to match the noun it belongs to,
the same way *this* does.

    mmɔ ákɛ́ bhɔ́ŋ            let the child not have     (mmɔ, child)
    mbwɛ́p ákɛ́ kɛ́m           let fever not catch        (mbwɛ́p, fever)
    nɛme nɛ́ndɔp              let the sickness remain    (nɛme, sickness)
    nɛnyɛ́ nɛ́ngo             let the food grow          (nɛnyɛ́, food)
    ɛnɛm ɛ́twó               the dry season comes       (ɛnɛm, dry season)
    ɛchɛmɛ ɛ́nkɔ             let the meeting go          (ɛchɛmɛ, meeting)

A noun starting nɛ- takes nɛ́-. A noun starting ɛ- takes ɛ́-. A person takes á-.
So the beginning of the noun and the beginning of the marker answer each other.

## ka marks you people

Wherever *you people* appears, it carries ka.

    ghók yɛ́ka               listen, you people
    sɛ́bhɨŋɨ beka            we are calling you people
    ɛywɛka abhɛ́n            those of you who
    Beka abhɛ́n báchí        those of you who are
    chí beka kɛ bǎyɛ́t       it is you people who bless

This is the same ka in the EyaLingo pronoun table, where bɛ́ka is *you* for more
than one and ɛ́yɛka is *your*.

## yɛ chí: if, even if

    Yɛ chí nɛme nɛ́twó                   if sickness is coming
    yɛ chí ɛnɛm ɛ́twó nɛ                 as dry season is approaching
    yɛ chí ngɔrɛ kɛ árɔ́ŋ ɛbhɨ           if a woman goes to farm
    yɛ chí mbakanɛm                      if a man goes to farm
    yɛ bátítí                            even if they are clearing
    yɛ chí nkwɔ́ ɛywɛka                   even those of you

With Mbák, which this file already has for *if*, there are two ways to open a
condition. Mbák chí ɛnɛm is *if it is dry season*; yɛ chí is used when one case
is being set beside another.

## mpok: a time for

mpok before a verb makes an occasion out of it. The speech lists the whole dry
season this way.

    mpok maŋák                  a time of happiness
    mpok babhɛ́n                 a time to dance
    mpok bɛgba                  a time to dance egba
    mpok bɛ́pók nsi              a time to bale out water for fish

## Saying it twice, again

    chókɔ́-chókɔ́            still alive, still sitting

With bataŋ-bataŋ and bɛtɔkɔ́-bɛtɔk from Text 1: doubling a word strengthens it
or spreads it out.

## Words this text confirms in use

    Ɛyú            yesterday            nɛbháŋ      harmattan
    ɛnɛm           dry season           mbwɛ́p       fever
    chay-nnya      convulsion           nɛme        sickness
    barɛ́m          ancestors            báyámbɨ     the elders
    ngɔrɛ          woman                mbakanɛm    man
    ɛbhɨ           bush, farm           nɛnyɛ́       food
    nsi            fish                 bagyɛ́p      palm trees
    ɛkak           leg                  sɛnɔŋ-áwɔ́   finger
    ambi           road                 mpok        time
    antɛn          below                awu         there
    ɛ̌tí            because of           maŋák       happiness
    ghók           listen, hear         kaká        thank

# Text 3: A journey

What the family says over someone who is about to travel. Corn is given to the
ancestors, the hands are named, and the evil is sent away ahead of him.

    Árɛ, ghó wɔ nɔ ɔ̌twɔ́ rɔ́ŋ nɛkɔ
    Arrey, you are going to travel

    Yíŋɨ́ wɔ nɛ nnɛrɛ́-ɛkɛt báyɛ́t bɛ́ áfɔ́k ámbák nɛ wɔ
    This is the family blessing that should follow you

    yíŋɨ́ nchwi nɛ bɛsɛ́ chyɛ ntá barɛ́m
    This is corn we are giving to our ancestors

    ɛnyiŋ ɛbúbú ɛ́kɛ́ koŋo wɔ
    no evil should come your way

    ɛnyiŋ ɛbúbú ɛ́mfwɛt nɔkɔ tɛ awu
    any evil should pass far away

    yíŋɨ́ awɔ́-nɛ́m nɛ awɔ́ ɛfa
    this is our right hand and the hand of luck

    áfɔ́k sɛ́chyɛ ntá barɛ́m mánnyɛ́
    we give blessings to ancestors to eat

    sɛ́rɛm ndu nnɛrɛ́-ɛkɛt bɛ́
    we are saying in the family that

    ɛnyiŋ ɛrɨ́rɨ́ kɛ ɛ́koŋo wɔ
    it is only good things that should follow you

    ɛnyiŋ ɛsɔ́ŋɔ́sɔ́ŋ ɛ́mfwɛ́t tɛ ɛbhɨ
    things that are difficult should pass in the bush

    annyú mfok chí ansɛm
    let him smell the dust behind

    ákɛ́ nyú ambi.
    and not in front

    Sɛ́ghɔ bɛ́ nɛkɔ ɛnɛ́n wɔ ɔ́rɔ́ŋ nɛ
    we have seen that in this journey

    ɔ́rɔ́ŋ kʉ chí beya bɛtɨk
    you are going to do a lot of work

# Text 4: Going to study abroad

Said over someone leaving to study. The same blessing shape as the journey, but
turned toward learning and toward what should come back.

    yɛ̌-ntiki ɛnyiŋ ɛnɛ́ ɔ́ghɔ́kɔ́
    everything that you study

    yɛ̌-ntiki ɛnyiŋ ɔ́tɔ́k awɔ́ arɛ́
    everything you lay your hands on

    ɔ́nsɔt chí awɔ́-nɛ́m
    you should take the right hand

    ɔ́mbɔŋ nɔkɔ mɛnyiŋ
    you should have a lot of property

    mɛnyiŋ mɛ́ntwɔ nɔkɔ beya-beya-beya.
    things should come to you in abundance

    Sɛ́tó wɔ ɔ́nchyɛ́ bɛsɛ́ nɛkwak bɛ́
    we are sending you to help us

    ntí ákɛ́ sáy wɔ
    you should not have any headache

    mmwɛ́t ákɛ́ bɛ́ wɔ
    you should not be sick

    ɔ́nkɔ kpɛrɛp.
    travel with confidence

# What Texts 3 and 4 teach

## The person markers, now a complete set

With ɔ́- for you, every person is accounted for. These are the markers that go
on the front of a verb.

    mmɛ, m-        I                   mmɛ nchí fyɛ́       I am putting in
    ɔ́-             you                 ɔ́rɔ́ŋ               you are going
    á-             he, she             ánsɔt              he should take
    sɛ́-            we                  sɛ́rɛm              we are saying
    bǎ-, mǎn-      you people          bǎghok             you have heard
    bá-, mán-      they                bátwɔ́              they have come

Text 4 is built almost entirely on ɔ́-:

    ɔ́ghɔ́kɔ́        you study           ɔ́nsɔt             you should take
    ɔ́tɔ́k           you lay             ɔ́mbɔŋ nɔkɔ        you should have
    ɔ́nchyɛ́         you should give     ɔ́nkɔ              you should go

## The marker answers the noun, and now the plural too

Each noun takes its own marker. Texts 3 and 4 add two more to the set.

    ɛnyiŋ ɛ́kɛ́ koŋo         let the thing not follow       ɛ- takes ɛ́-
    mɛnyiŋ mɛ́ntwɔ nɔkɔ      let the things keep coming     mɛ- takes mɛ́-
    áfɔ́k ámbák nɛ wɔ        let the blessing be with you   á- takes á-
    nɛnyɛ́ nɛ́ngo            let the food grow              nɛ- takes nɛ́-
    ɛchɛmɛ ɛ́nkɔ            let the meeting go             ɛ- takes ɛ́-
    mmɔ ákɛ́ bhɔ́ŋ           let the child not have         person takes á-

So ɛnyiŋ, one thing, takes ɛ́-, and mɛnyiŋ, many things, takes mɛ́-. The marker
follows the noun into the plural.

## kɛ: it is this one that

Three texts now show kɛ marking the thing being singled out. The pattern is
noun, then kɛ, then the verb.

    ɛnyiŋ ɛrɨ́rɨ́ kɛ ɛ́koŋo wɔ        it is GOOD things that follow you
    chí beka kɛ bǎyɛ́t                it is YOU PEOPLE who bless
    yɛ chí ngɔrɛ kɛ árɔ́ŋ ɛbhɨ        if it is a WOMAN who goes to farm

This is separate from Kɛ at the head of a line, which means *but*.

## yíŋɨ́: here is, look

A third way of pointing at something, beside Ghó in Text 1 and Fá chí in the
scripture work.

    Yíŋɨ́ wɔ nɛ nnɛrɛ́-ɛkɛt báyɛ́t    here is the family blessing
    yíŋɨ́ nchwi                       here is corn
    yíŋɨ́ awɔ́-nɛ́m                   here is our right hand

This is the yɨ́ŋɨ already in the word bank, meaning to look.

## Saying it three times

    beya-beya-beya          in abundance

beya on its own is *many*. Doubled it would be more. Tripled it is abundance.
With bataŋ-bataŋ and chókɔ́-chókɔ́ from the earlier texts, repetition builds
strength step by step.

## ɛrɨ́rɨ́: good, from ɛ́rɨ doubled

    ɛnyiŋ ɛrɨ́rɨ́            good things
    ɛnyiŋ ɛbúbú             evil things
    ɛnyiŋ ɛsɔ́ŋɔ́sɔ́ŋ        difficult things

The pattern is the same each time: ɛnyiŋ, then what kind of thing. And ɛrɨ́rɨ́
is the ɛ́rɨ already in this file, doubled.

## tɛ: as far as

    ɛ́mfwɛt nɔkɔ tɛ awu          let it pass far away, as far as over there
    ɛ́mfwɛ́t tɛ ɛbhɨ             let it pass as far as the bush

This is the tɛ already in this file for *until*. Sending trouble *as far as the
bush* is how it is said.

## yɛ̌-ntiki: every, everything

    yɛ̌-ntiki ɛnyiŋ ɛnɛ́ ɔ́ghɔ́kɔ́      everything that you study
    yɛ̌-ntiki ɛnyiŋ ɔ́tɔ́k awɔ́ arɛ́     everything you lay your hands on

This is the yɛ-ntɨkɨ already in this file for *every*.

## Words these texts confirm in use

    nɛkɔ          journey              nchwi        corn
    barɛ́m         ancestors            áfɔ́k        blessing
    ɛfa           luck                 mfok         dust
    nnɛrɛ́-ɛkɛt    family               nɛkwak       help
    ɛnyiŋ         thing                mɛnyiŋ       things
    koŋo          follow               bɛtɨk        work

# What Text 5 teaches

## The nasal in the marker changes to match the next sound

This explains something that has looked inconsistent since Text 1. The marker
mán- is sometimes written mám-, and á- is sometimes ám- and sometimes án-. It is
not carelessness. The nasal takes the shape of the sound that follows it.

**m before the lip sounds b, p and f:**

    Má-bɛp      becomes   Mámbɛp          they will ask
    mǎ-pɔkɔ     becomes   mǎmpɔkɔ         you should carry
    á-bak       becomes   ámbak           it should be
    ɛ́-fú       becomes   ɛ́mfú           it should come
    á-pɛrɛ      becomes   amperɛ          he comes back

**n before everything else:**

    Mán-twó     stays     Mántwó          let them come
    Mán-sɔt     stays     Mánsɔt          let them take
    á-sɔt       becomes   ánsɔt           he should take
    ɛ́-chwe     becomes   ɛ́nchwe         it should enter
    mǎn-jiŋɨ    stays     mǎnjiŋɨ         you should care for
    mɛ́-twɔ     becomes   mɛ́ntwɔ         they should come

Across five texts there is no example of m before a non-lip sound, or n before a
lip sound. So when building a word with one of these markers, look at the sound
that follows and write m or n to match it.

## kpát tɛ: until

    kpát tɛ yí ágháká awu           until he reaches there
    kpát tɛ yí amperɛ-nsɛm          until he comes back
    kpát tɛ bɛsɛ́ babhɔŋ sɛ́nka     until we all agree

This joins kpátɛ, which this file already has for *except*, and tɛ for *until*.

## chí: only this and nothing else

Placed after the verb, chí narrows it to one thing. The line that follows then
shuts out the opposite.

    Ánnyú mfok chí ansɛm            let him breathe dust ONLY behind
    ákɛ́ nyú ambi                   let him not breathe it in front

    ánjat chí kebí                  let him step ONLY on faeces
    ákɛ́ yat nse                    let him not step on thorns

    ansɔt chí awɔ́-nɛ́m             let him take ONLY the right hand
    ákɛ́ sɔt awɔ́-ghɔ               let him not take the left

    angɔ́ chí bɛsɛ́                 let him see ONLY us
    ákɛ́ ghɔ́ ɛnyiŋ ɛnɛ́ ɛbúbú      let him not see evil

This is the shape of a blessing: say the good thing, then shut the door on its
opposite. Four times in this one speech.

## kɛ singles out, a fourth time

    ě aywɛsɛ́ mmɔ kɛ ábhɔ́ŋ bɛ́kóŋo yí
    it is OUR CHILD that has to follow

With the three from the earlier texts, the pattern holds: noun, then kɛ, then
the verb.

## Speaking to the ancestors

The whole speech is built on one arrangement. The living address the dead as
*you people*, and talk about the traveller as *him*.

    mǎnjiŋɨ nɔkɔ yí         YOU should take care of HIM
    mǎmpɔkɔ yí pɛtii        YOU should carry HIM

    ánsáy yí                let it pain him
    ákɛ́ nyú ambi           let him not breathe in front
    ágháká awu              until he reaches there

So mǎn- for the ancestors being asked, and á- for the boy being spoken about.

    ɛtɔk barɨk   abroad                      ndɨk        white person
    nɛmɔ         exam, test                  nɛbhay      marriage
    ɛkáti        book, school                kebí        faeces
    nse          thorns                      mfok        dust
    ammɨk        below, on the ground        awu         over there
    ágháká       reaches                     amperɛ-nsɛm comes back
    ɛ́kway       is good, is enough          ɛ́mɔt       one

# Text 6: The morning prayer

Not a blessing spoken over someone else, but a man speaking for himself. This is
why it matters: every other text says *let him* and *let them*, and this one says
*I*. It is where the first person lives.

**How it is done.** When an elder gets up in the village, he puts water in the
clay pot and starts looking at the direction of the sun. He will wash his hands
and face and starts reciting:

    Ndu mmu nsɛ́nsí ɛtɔk áfaté bɛtí, ǎfyɛ́ manyɛp ndu ɛte ntɔp anjɛ́t
    anjiŋi nɔkɔ ɛbhak mmok áfú anso amɔ́ ambɨk nɔkɔ

## Waking

    Ɛtá Mandɛm, ɛ́yú mbɨrɛ kɛnɔ́
    My Lord, yesterday, I slept

    kɛnɔ́ kɛ́ chí nɛwú.
    that sleep was death

    Nɛ́nɛ ḿfáté
    right now, I have woken up

    chí bɛtaŋ ɛbhɛ́n wɔ ɔ́chyɛ́ mmɛ kɛ ḿfáté
    It is by your strength that I have woken up

    mbɔnyunɛ́ kɛnɔ́ chí manɔ nɛwú.
    because sleep is the brother of death

    Kɛ ndu wɔ ɔ́kú mmɛ ḿfáté,
    But, as you have allowed me to wake up

    nchí kaka wɔ.
    I am thanking you

## Asking for the day

    Yɛ̌ mmɛ mfu chí nɛfí
    Even if I go out

    ɔ́kɛ́ ka mmɛ ńtɛ́m ɛkak ndu ntay
    do not allow me to hit my leg on a stone

    yɛ̌ ndɔŋ chí ɛbhɨ,
    Even if I go to the bush

    ɔ́kɛ́ ka nnyɔ́ ámfú mmɛ kɛ́kwɔ́t mmwɛ́t
    don't allow a snake to come near me

    yɛ̌ mmɛ ndɔŋ chí kɛntɛ́mɛ́,
    Even if I go hunting

    nnya amfu nɔkɔ mmɛ bɛsí.
    let animals appear in front of me

## Asking to be held to his own heart

    Kɛ Ɛtá ndu mmɛ ghati wɔ mɛnyiŋ ɛbhɛn,
    However Father, as I am telling you these things

    ndu mmɛ nchí nɛ
    as you know me

    mmɛ ńgɔ́ mmɔ mmu ndu áfwɛ́t,
    if I see somebody's child passing by

    ńjíŋí amík abú-ábú
    I look at him with evil intentions

    ɔ́kɛ́ ka nɛ mmɛ.
    do not agree with me

    Ḿbák ńchɔ́kɔ́ ɛrɨ́tɨ́ ɛnyiŋ mmɛ mbɛ́ mmɛ kɛ nkwáy bɛ́bhɔ́ŋ yɔ́,
    If I have somebody's thing, I get angry that it is only me to have it

    Ɛtá ɔ́kɛ́ ka nɛ mmɛ.
    Father, do not agree with me

## But if his heart is good

    Kɛ, ḿbák mmɛ ngɔ ɛnyiŋ tɔkɔ́ mmú mbɛ́ ɛ́ndɔ́k ɛrɨ́rɨ́
    but, if I see somebody's thing and wish blessings

    ngɔ mmɔ mmu ḿpwɛ bɛ́ mbɛ́ mmɔ awa
    if I see somebody's child and say it is my child

    ángɔ́ ámbák nnɛ́m mmɔ́
    let him grow and become great

    ngɔ ngɔrɛ mmu, ámfwɛ́t mbɛ́ eé
    I see a woman and I wish her to be a mother of children

    mámkwak yí ambé bó
    let them help her to bear children

    Ḿbák mbɔŋ chí nti nɔ,
    if I possess such a heart

    Ɛtá ɔkway bɛ́kwak mmɛ ḿbɔ́ŋ bɔ́.
    Father, help me to have children

    Kwak mmɛ bɛ́ mmɔ awa ákɛ́ me.
    help me so that my child should not be sick

    kwak mmɛ bɛ́ mmu nnɛrɛ́-ɛkɛt awa ákɛ́ me
    help me so that none of my family members should be sick

    mbɔ nti ɛna nɛ́chí fú
    because I have a plain heart

    bɛ́ nnɛrɛ́-ɛkɛt ánchák nkɛm.
    that the family should increase

    Ɛtá nkáká wɔ tontó.
    Father, many thanks to You

# What Text 6 teaches

## I is ḿ- or ń-, and the nasal rule holds

Every other text speaks about other people. This one speaks for the man himself,
so it is where the first person finally appears, and it follows the same sound
rule found in Text 5.

**m before the lip sounds:**

    ḿfáté           I have woken up
    ḿbák            if I
    ḿpwɛ            I say
    ḿbɔ́ŋ           I have
    mbɨrɛ           I slept

**n before everything else:**

    ńtɛ́m           I hit
    ńgɔ́            I see
    ńchɔ́kɔ́        I have
    ńjíŋí           I look
    nchí            I am
    nkáká           I thank
    nkwáy           I can

So the nasal rule covers every person in the language. Twenty-three examples
across the texts, with no exception.

## ka: to allow, to agree

This settles a word that has appeared since Text 1 without a meaning.

    ɔ́kɛ́ ka mmɛ ńtɛ́m ɛkak           do not ALLOW me to hit my leg
    ɔ́kɛ́ ka nnyɔ́ ámfú mmɛ          do not ALLOW a snake to come near me
    ɔ́kɛ́ ka nɛ mmɛ                  do not AGREE with me
    sɛ́nka bɛ́                       until we AGREE that

So in Text 5, bakɛ́ ka ntí ánsáy yí is *let them not allow the head to pain him*.
One word, and it carries both allowing and agreeing.

## The shape of a plea: do not allow me to

    ɔ́kɛ́ ka + person + verb

    ɔ́kɛ́ ka mmɛ ńtɛ́m ɛkak ndu ntay      do not allow ME to hit MY leg
    ɔ́kɛ́ ka nnyɔ́ ámfú mmɛ              do not allow a SNAKE to come near me

The one being stopped comes after ka, and the verb then carries that person's
own marker: mmɛ with ń-, nnyɔ́ with á-.

## mbɔnyunɛ́: because

    mbɔnyunɛ́ kɛnɔ́ chí manɔ nɛwú      because sleep is the brother of death

## Yɛ̌ ... chí: even if

    Yɛ̌ mmɛ mfu chí nɛfí               even if I go out
    yɛ̌ ndɔŋ chí ɛbhɨ                  even if to the bush
    yɛ̌ mmɛ ndɔŋ chí kɛntɛ́mɛ́         even if I go hunting

With Ḿbák for a plain *if*, there are two kinds of condition: Ḿbák sets a case,
and Yɛ̌ ... chí grants it in advance, the way *even if* does.

## The marker answers the noun, now for kɛ- words too

    kɛnɔ́ kɛ́ chí nɛwú           sleep, it is death

So kɛnɔ́ takes kɛ́, alongside ɛnyiŋ taking ɛ́, mɛnyiŋ taking mɛ́, and nɛnyɛ́
taking nɛ́. The marker is the beginning of the noun said again.

## kɛ singles out, in prayer too

    chí bɛtaŋ ɛbhɛ́n wɔ ɔ́chyɛ́ mmɛ kɛ ḿfáté
    it is YOUR STRENGTH that woke me

    mbɛ́ mmɛ kɛ nkwáy bɛ́bhɔ́ŋ yɔ́
    that it is ONLY ME who can have it

## Saying it twice, for evil

    amík abú-ábú            eyes full of evil

With ɛbúbú for evil in Text 3, and ɛrɨ́rɨ́ for good, the doubling runs through
the whole vocabulary of good and bad.

## Words this text confirms in use

    kɛnɔ́        sleep                  nɛwú        death
    bɛtaŋ       strength               manɔ        brother
    ntay        stone                  nnyɔ́       snake
    nnya        animal                 ɛbhɨ        bush
    nɛfí        outside                bɛsí        face, in front
    nti         heart                  bó          children
    ngɔrɛ       woman                  nnɛrɛ́-ɛkɛt family
    kwak        help                   kaka        thank
    ɛte ntɔp    clay pot               mmok        sun

# What Text 7 teaches

## There is no word for and between two verbs

Where English joins two actions with *and*, Kenyang says the person marker again
on the second verb. The marker is repeated, not a joining word.

    sɛ́nkwáy sɛ́nkáká yí          let us sing, let us praise her
    sɛ́nkwáy sɛ́nsyɛ́ptí yí       let us sing, let us welcome him
    mantwɔ́ mangók                let them come, let them hear
    ɛ́ngɔ́ ɛ́kɛ́ bhɔ́ŋ ɛsɔŋɔri    let it grow, let it not have trouble

The earlier texts do the same, which means this is how the language works and
not a habit of one speaker:

    Mánsɔt mmɛm amɛn mánnyú      let them take these drinks, let them drink
    Sɛ́nku sɛ́mbɛ́n babhɛ́n       let us rise, let us dance

So to say *come and eat*, do not look for a word for *and*. Say the marker
twice.

## The marker answers the noun, again

    nɛme nɛ́kɛ́taŋ               let the sickness not be hard     nɛ- takes nɛ́-
    ɛnyiŋ ɛ́ngɔ́                 let the thing grow               ɛ- takes ɛ́-
    manɛ́m mántwɔ nɔkɔ           let the boys keep coming         ma- takes mán-

With ɛnyiŋ taking ɛ́-, mɛnyiŋ taking mɛ́-, nɛnyɛ́ taking nɛ́-, and kɛnɔ́ taking
kɛ́-, the rule holds through every text so far: the marker is the beginning of
the noun said again.

## Naming the ancestors by where they are

The speech does not call the dead with one word. It names each place they may be,
and calls them separately.

    abhɛ́n bachí awu ammɨk                     those of you below
    bɔ ɛ́tá ɛywɛsɛ́ abhɛ́n báchí amfay         our fathers who are above
    barɛ́m abhɛ́n báchí bɛtɔk bɔ ɛ́tá ɛywɛsɛ́  the ancestors in our fathers' villages
    abhɛ́n báchí amɛm nchɔ́t                   those who are in the forest

abhɛ́n báchí is *those who are*, and what follows says where. Below, above, in
the villages, in the forest. Nobody is left out.

## The order asked for in children

    abhɛ́n baghɔrɛ mánkono nɔkɔ      let the female children follow
    bɛfák mɛ́ntwɔ́                   let twin children come forth

With kɛ́nkono for *follow*, the same koŋo already in this file.

## Tanyi and Manyi: what twins make of their parents

    bɛfák mɛ́ntwɔ́                   let twin children come forth
    nnɛ́m ɛyi ambak Tanyi            let the father be Tanyi
    yí ambak Mányi                  let the wife be Manyi

These lines follow the asking for twins, so Tanyi and Manyi are what the parents
of twins become. They are not ordinary names: they are earned. Both are in the
list of Bayangi family names, which is why so many people carry them.

# What Text 8 teaches

## Dying is spoken of as travelling

The title of this song is Nɛwú, death. Inside the song that word is never said
once. What is said instead is that he has gone on a journey.

    Nnɛ́m arɔŋ nɛkɔ.            My husband has travelled
    Ngɔbhu arɔŋ nɛkɔ            Nursing mother has travelled

nɛkɔ is the same word Text 3 uses for a real journey, where the family blesses a
man who is about to travel. The living are sent off with corn and blessings; the
dead are spoken of the same way.

So when teaching nɛkɔ, teach both: a journey a man takes, and the journey he does
not come back from.

## kʉ: to do

    Nnɛ́m awa akʉ bɛtɨk ndɛ́m.      my husband has done work for nothing
    Sɛ́kʉ bɛtɨk ndɛ́m.              we have done work for nothing
    Bén akʉ bɛtɨk ndɛ́ndɛ́m         Ben has done work in vain

With Text 3's ɔ́rɔ́ŋ kʉ chí beya bɛtɨk, *you are going to do a lot of work*, this
is the same verb. To work is to do work: kʉ bɛtɨk.

## Doubling, for grief

    ndɛ́m               in vain
    ndɛ́ndɛ́m           in vain, said of Ben

The same doubling that strengthens bataŋ-bataŋ and ɛrɨ́rɨ́ is used here to make a
loss heavier.

## The cry at the end of a line

In this song a line often ends in á, é or ó, and sometimes opens with O ó. Where
these appear, the English carries an *oh*.

    Nnɛ́m arɔŋ nɛkɔ á.          Oh, my husband has travelled
    Nnɛ́m awa é.                oh, my husband
    O ó nnɛ́m awa é             Oh, my husband

They are not words with meanings. They are the sound of the crying, written down.

## Ɔ́n-: you should, said to the dead

    Ɔ́nkaka mamí awa.           you should greet my mother
    Ɔ́nkaka mmɛ Tambɛ.          you should greet Tambe for me
    Ɔ́nkaka bo mankɛm           greet everybody

This is the ɔ́- for *you* from Texts 3 and 4, with n before k exactly as the sound
rule says. What is striking is who it is addressed to. She is still speaking to
him, and still asking him to do something.

## Bayangi names, written in Kenyang

The song names real people, and shows how the family names are written in Kenyang
rather than in English spelling.

    Ayók            Ayuk
    Tambɛ           Tambe
    Sofí            Sophie
    Matá            Martha
    Bén             Ben

Ayuk and Tambe are both in the list of Bayangi family names.

## Words this text confirms in use

    nɛwú        death                 nɛkɔ        journey
    nnɛ́m        husband               bɔ          children
    bɛtɨk       work                  kʉ          to do
    rɔ          to leave              kaka        to greet
    mmá         mother                ɛ́tá        father
    bo mankɛm   everybody             awu         over there

# What Text 9 teaches

## A condition can be made without any word for if

This file already has Ḿbák for *if* and Yɛ̌ ... chí for *even if*. The blessing
over the farm uses neither. It simply says what a man does, then says what should
follow, and the two clauses sitting side by side carry the condition.

    Mmu ánkʉ mmɛ́n, mmɛ́n ɛyɔ ambɛ́ chí bɛfák
    if somebody buys a goat, let the goat deliver twins

    apɨ chí nchwi ankyɛ́p ampati amfay ɛkɛt ɛ́njwi
    if he plants corn, let it fill the barn

    akwaŋ chí bako, ankɔ́k mɔ́ tontó
    if he plants beans, let him have a good harvest

    Aku nkɔk, nkɔk ambyɛ́rɛ́-byɛ́t
    if he buys a fowl, let it grow fat

The first clause is a plain statement with its person marker. The second carries
the *let* marker. No joining word stands between them.

## The whole blessing runs on one shape

Every line of the farm blessing names the thing, then blesses that same thing by
name. The noun is said twice, and the second time it takes its marker.

    mmɛ́n ... mmɛ́n ɛyɔ ambɛ́          the goat ... let that goat bear
    nkɨ ... nkɨ ɛwu ɛrɨ́rɨ́ ɛ́mfu      the farm ... let that farm yield good
    nkɔk ... nkɔk ambyɛ́rɛ́-byɛ́t      the fowl ... let that fowl grow fat

ɛyɔ and ɛwu are *that one*, pointing back at what was just named.

## chí marks the thing itself

In Text 5 chí narrowed a blessing to one thing and shut out its opposite. Here it
points at what exactly is planted or taken.

    apɨ chí nchwi               he plants, namely corn
    akwaŋ chí bako              he farms, namely beans
    Apɨ chí bɛ́kwá              he plants, namely plantains
    sɛ́nsɔt nɔkɔ chí awɔ́-nɛ́m   let us take, namely the right hand

## The numbers, alive in a sentence

    Yí nkwɔ́ manywɔp tándrámɔt, byó nɛ ánwi
    He should follow after seven days, after fourteen days

tándrámɔt is seven, and byó nɛ ánwi is ten and four. This is the first time the
counting appears in real speech rather than in a list, and it confirms that
bigger numbers are built with nɛ, and.

## ka: to accept, again

    mmu ngo ambɨŋɨ yí anka
    if the big man calls him, let him agree

This is the ka of Text 6, to allow or to agree, now shown as something a man does
when he is called.

## Saying it twice, for growing fat

    ambyɛ́rɛ́-byɛ́t          let it grow fat

Beside bataŋ-bataŋ for strong drink and chɔ́kɔ́-chɔ́kɔ́ for still living.

## bɛtik ndɨ́k: the white man's work

    abhɛ́n bákʉ bɛtik ndɨ́k          those in the Whiteman's job

ndɨk is a white person. So salaried work, with exams and a boss and a pay packet,
is named *the white man's work*, and it stands apart from bɛtik alone, which is
the work of the farm. The speech blesses both, in turn: first the farm, then the
office.

## Calling the dead by where they lie

The speech does not call the ancestors with one word. It names every place they
may be, so that none is left out.

    abhɛ́n ammɨk                 those below
    abhɛ́n awu amfay             those above
    barɛ́m abhɛ́n annyɛ́n         the ancestors in the river
    abhɛ́n amfay njiyɛ           those in the hills
    abhɛ́n nchɔ́t                those in the forest
    abhɛ́n báchí ngɔk nnyɛ́n     those at the river banks
