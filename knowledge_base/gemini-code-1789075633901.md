## 2. History of Tone Orthography & The Apostrophe 
The rules for marking tone in Kenyang have evolved to make reading more fluid for native speakers.

### The 1989 System vs. Modern Revisions
* The previous Kenyang orthography, proposed by Tanyi Mbuagbaw in 1989, included tone marks for high [´], falling [^], rising [ˇ] and mid [¯], while low tone was left unmarked[cite: 14].
* Readers and teachers noted significant difficulties with the falling and mid tone marks[cite: 14].
* Dr. Keith Snider and Tanyi Mbuagbaw later determined that the "mid tone" was actually a downstepped-high caused by an intervening "floating" low tone[cite: 14]. 
* Snider originally proposed using an apostrophe followed by a high mark (e.g., *bá'pá*) to represent this downstepped-high[cite: 14].
* **Community Rejection:** During testing in Bachuo-Ntai and Besongabang, there was an expressed general resistance to the use of the apostrophe in the orthography[cite: 14]. Native speakers argued that the context of the sentence would suffice for differentiating between simple past and progressive verb forms[cite: 14].
* As a result, the primer was taught without the apostrophe[cite: 14]. Instead, to avoid ambiguity, the grave accent (`è`) on the first syllable of the verb root was proposed as the "progressive" mark (e.g., *Bó bátè eket* = 'They are building a house')[cite: 14].

### Testing Recommendations for Kenyang Orthography
For future testing of Kenyang orthography, linguists recommended the following standards[cite: 14]:
* The sample size should be no less than 50 participants[cite: 14].
* Tests should use comparison groups (e.g., teaching one group different tone markings than the other)[cite: 14].
* Tests must consist of both reading proficiency (reading unseen material aloud) and writing proficiency (a dictation exercise)[cite: 14].

## 3. Advanced Noun Phrase Structures

### Strict Word Order in Kenyang
Kenyang is a head-initial language, meaning the head noun generally comes first[cite: 16]. All modifying elements must follow the head noun in this exact sequence[cite: 16]:

1. **Noun** (e.g., *be-ket* / 'houses')[cite: 16]
2. **AP** (Association Particle: *έ*)[cite: 16]
3. **Attributive Noun** (e.g., *be-rórí* / 'length')[cite: 16]
4. **AP** (*í*)[cite: 16]
5. **Adjective** (e.g., *pépέp* / 'white')[cite: 16]
6. **AP** (*έ*)[cite: 16]
7. **Possessive** (e.g., *b-a* / 'my')[cite: 16]
8. **Relative Clause** (e.g., *e-bhén chu* / 'which are red')[cite: 16]
9. **Demonstrative** (e.g., *bo* / 'those')[cite: 16]
10. **Quantifier** (e.g., *me-nkem* / 'all')[cite: 16]
11. **Numeral** (e.g., *bá-pay* / 'two')[cite: 16]

### Indefinite Pronoun Paradigm
Indefinite pronouns in Kenyang are constructed by combining a base word (person, thing, time, place) with a modifier (some, any/every, no)[cite: 16].

| Base Category | "Some-" (+ fú) | "Any- / Every-" (+ yěntiki / nkem) | "No / Never" (+ yě) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Human (mmu)** | mmu-ǎ-fu (Someone)[cite: 16] | yěntiki-mmu / bo-mankem (Anyone/Everyone)[cite: 16] | yě-mmu (No one)[cite: 16] |
| **Thing (enyin)** | enyin-i-fú (Something)[cite: 16] | yěntiki-nyin / menyíŋ-menkem (Anything/Everything)[cite: 16] | yέ-enyíŋ (Nothing)[cite: 16] |
| **Time (mpok)** | mpok-ɔ-fu (Sometime)[cite: 16] | yěntiki-mpok / mpók-nkem (Anytime/Always)[cite: 16] | yě-mpok (Never)[cite: 16] |
| **Place (nebhwét)** | nebhwét-é-fu (Somewhere)[cite: 16] | yěntiki-nebhwét / mbăn-nkem (Anywhere/Everywhere)[cite: 16] | yě-nebhwét (Nowhere)[cite: 16] |

### Noun Class "Residue" and Collapse
Linguistic research indicates that the Kenyang noun class system may be slowly disintegrating or shifting[cite: 16]:
* **Class 1 and Class 3 Collapse:** Class 1 and Class 3 seem to share the exact same concord system[cite: 16]. It is highly possible they were historically distinct classes separated by tone, but due to a gradual loss of tonal distinctions, they have become nearly indistinguishable[cite: 16].
* **Nasal Prefix Confusion:** When evaluating nouns with a nasal prefix, speakers often exhibit inconsistency[cite: 16]. Depending on dialectal or generational differences, some speakers will assign Class 3 concord to these nouns, while others will assign Class 5 concord[cite: 16].
